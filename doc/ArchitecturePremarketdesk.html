doc / architecture

PremarketDesk

A program that watches the American stock market in the hours before it opens, and emails one page at 08:46 naming the shares that have already moved overnight and what is behind each move. It runs on a single Windows PC. There is no server, no website and no company: seven scheduled tasks wake on their own clock times, each doing one job and leaving files behind for the next. The schedule is the architecture.

This page describes how it is built. Sections 01 and 02 assume nothing and are written for a reader who has never traded a share or read a line of code. Everything from 03 on gets progressively more technical, and the last four sections are lookup tables rather than reading.

01  Plain words

What this actually does

The problem

The American stock market trades from 09:30 to 16:00 New York time. But shares do not sit still overnight. A company reports its results at 07:00, or gets taken over, or a regulator approves its drug, and by the time the market opens its shares are worth fifteen percent more than they were at yesterday's close. That jump is called a gap.

Roughly forty or fifty shares gap meaningfully on an ordinary morning, out of several thousand. Finding out which ones, and why each one moved, is the hour between about 07:00 and 09:30 that people who trade spend clicking through screens and news feeds.

What it does about it

At 08:46, forty four minutes before the market opens, an email arrives. It names the shares that have moved, quotes the headline behind each one, and says whether the buying and selling behind the move is unusually heavy for that particular share or perfectly ordinary. Then it says where the price sits against a few reference levels that matter to someone deciding whether to act.

That last distinction is most of the value. A share up eight percent on ordinary volume and a share up eight percent on forty times its usual volume look identical on a list of movers and are not remotely the same event.

The part that matters most

Every morning the system writes down what it claimed. Every night it goes back and writes down what actually happened. Those two records sit side by side and never on top of each other.

This is the real project. The rules it screens on today were copied from a third party and have never been tested on this data, so at the moment the email is a well formatted list of shares that met a stranger's rules. Only after enough recorded mornings can anyone ask whether those rules are worth anything at all. The daily briefing exists partly to be useful and partly to keep the thing running long enough for that record to become large enough to answer the question.

Which is why nothing here is ever quietly corrected. When the night finds that the morning was wrong about a level, it writes the true value in a new column beside the old one. The distance between the two is not a mistake to be tidied away, it is the measurement.

02  Terminology

Terminology

Everything below this section uses these words without stopping to explain them. There are about twenty, and they are the whole vocabulary. The report the system emails carries its own version of this list at the bottom of the page, generated from one file so a word cannot come to mean two things in two places.

Premarket

The hours before the market officially opens, from 04:00 to 09:30 New York time. Trading does happen, in much smaller amounts and by fewer people. This is the window the whole system listens to.

The open, the bell

09:30 New York time, when normal trading starts. The close is 16:00. A session is one such day.

Gap

The difference between where a share finished yesterday and where it is trading this morning, as a percentage. A share that closed at 100 and is at 112 before the open has gapped up twelve percent.

Prior close

Yesterday's final price. Every gap is measured against it, which is why getting the right one, from the right day, matters more than it sounds.

Volume

How many shares changed hands. High volume means a lot of people acted; low volume means the price moved on very little trading and may not hold.

Relative volume RVOL

Today's premarket volume divided by what that same share normally does by the same time of morning. A value of 1 is a typical morning for it. A value of 40 means something is happening. This is the single most useful number the report carries, because it is the one that separates a real event from a twitch.

Catalyst

The reason a share moved: an earnings report, a takeover, a drug approval, an analyst upgrade. If no news explains a move, the report says so rather than inventing a reason.

Earnings

A company's quarterly results announcement. The most common reason a share gaps, and scheduled in advance, which is why the system can look tomorrow's reporters up rather than waiting to be surprised.

Market capitalisation market cap

What the whole company is worth: share price times the number of shares. Used here mainly to tell a large stable company from a tiny volatile one.

Float

How many shares are actually available to trade, as opposed to locked up with founders and insiders. A small float means a modest amount of buying can move the price a long way.

The universe

The roughly 2,750 shares this system will consider at all, rebuilt every Sunday. Ordinary company shares only, above a minimum size and price, with enough trading history to measure anything about. Everything else is out of scope permanently.

The pool, the watchlist

The much shorter list, built at 03:55 and again at 07:15, of names worth listening to this particular morning. It is a guess made before any of today's prices exist, from who reports today, what was in the news overnight, and who moved yesterday.

The collector

The part that sits and listens to live trades from 04:00 to 10:30 and writes down a summary of each minute. It is the only source of today's premarket prices, and what it fails to hear is gone permanently, because a live market cannot be replayed afterwards.

Candidate

A share that made the pool, was listened to, and turned out to have actually moved. Twelve of these reach the report each morning.

Premarket high and low pm_high, pm_low

The highest and lowest prices seen during the premarket. The report prints them because they are the obvious levels a trader would watch, not because it suggests acting on them.

Entry and stop reference

Two levels printed beside each name: where a trade would begin if someone took it, and where they would abandon it. They are recorded so the system can grade itself later against what it actually said. They are not advice.

Conviction score

A number from 0 to 10 assembled from the gap size, the relative volume, the kind of catalyst, and where the price sits. Bucketed green, yellow or red. Every component is stored separately, so the score can be argued with later rather than trusted.

Paper trade

A trade recorded on paper only, with no money involved, so the rule behind it can be judged after a few hundred of them. The rule was written down before any of the code existed, which is what stops it being quietly reshaped to flatter the results.

Backfill

Going back at night to fill in what was not knowable in the morning. The data vendor publishes complete records of the premarket hours only several hours after they happen.

The vendor EODHD

The company this system buys its market data from. Every number that reaches the email comes from them, so there is never a question of which source a figure came from. A second vendor is used at night, but only to check the record afterwards, and it can never reach a report.

About the C-numbers. From section 04 onward the diagrams and the reference tables at the back label each part with a code like C16 or C59. They are just short names for the seventy moving parts, so that a box in a diagram, a row in a table and a line in the file map can be matched up without ambiguity. Section 13 lists every one of them. You can ignore them entirely and lose nothing, and the prose is written so that you can: sentences name the file, as scan.py or true_volume.py, and leave the codes to the pictures. Until 2026-09-06 several paragraphs used a code as the subject of the sentence, which made this promise false wherever it mattered most.

03  Purpose

What this is for

The same thing section 01 said, stated precisely. PremarketDesk has two goals that live on different timescales, and most of the confusion about it comes from reading one and expecting the other.

The daily goal is orientation before the bell. Between 08:46 and 08:49 an email arrives naming the stocks that gapped overnight, the headline behind each one, whether the premarket volume behind the move is unusual for that ticker or ordinary, and where price sits against the prior high, the premarket high, and the 200-day average. It replaces the hour most people spend scanning between 07:00 and 09:30. It is not a signal and cannot be one, because gap setups resolve in the first fifteen minutes and the report is read at 09:00.

The long goal is finding out whether the screen is worth anything. Every morning the system writes down what it claimed and where the levels were. Every night it writes down what actually happened. After enough sessions that record answers questions that cannot be answered today: whether gap plus catalyst plus premarket relative volume has an edge on this data rather than on the data of whoever published the thresholds, whether a high confluence score outperforms a low one, and which component of the score is carrying the result. Until then the thresholds in CRITERIA.md are seed values copied from a third party and the report is a well-formatted list of names that met a stranger's rules.

The briefing exists to keep the system in daily use long enough for the record to become worth reading. That is the whole design in one sentence.

This page describes the system as it stands and nothing else. What changed on which date is in CHANGELOG.md, why a choice that could have gone the other way went the way it did is in DECISIONS.md, and every threshold is in CRITERIA.md. Where a date appears below it is attached to a MEASUREMENT, because a measurement without the day it was taken is worth less than one with it.

Sample unit is the session, not the row. Twelve candidates from one morning share a market, a macro print, and often a sector move, so they are not twelve independent observations. Any analysis of the record aggregates to one figure per session before comparing conviction buckets.

04  Timeline

The weekday, end to end

In plain words

A day in the life. Sunday night the system decides which few thousand shares it will consider all week. At four in the morning it starts listening to live trading. At quarter to nine it stops, works out what it heard, and decides which twelve names are worth writing about. At twelve it checks how the morning's names actually did. At ten past ten at night, once the data provider has finally published the complete record of the morning, it goes back and writes down what was really true.

Seven jobs, none of which ever speaks to another. Each leaves files behind and the next one picks them up. The only exception is a watchdog that checks whether the others ran and restarts the ones that are safe to restart.

Each job wakes on its clock time, does one thing, and exits. No job in the data path calls another. What passes between them is files on disk, which is why a dead job shows up downstream as a missing file with a stated reason rather than as a crash.

The one exception is the watchdog (tasks/job_monitor.bat, registered as monitor, one task with three triggers: every 30 minutes for two hours from 07:25, every 30 minutes for one hour from 12:25, and once at 22:45, with the pass decided from the clock in ops/monitor_jobs.py). It reads Task Scheduler's own record for each job alongside that job's dated log, and relaunches the job's .bat when the job fired and died, when it never fired at all because the machine was asleep, when no collector is alive inside the collector window, or when universe.json has gone past universe_rerun_after_days = 8. Only idempotent work is rerun, at most max_reruns_per_job_per_day = 1 per job per day so a hard failure cannot loop, and never a second collector while one is still writing bars, because two collectors folding the same tape write duplicate minutes and double the premarket volume downstream. The 12:00 pass is the one job it watches and never relaunches. That sweep spends about 2,902 credits on the shared key, and job_midday.bat stamps PMD_JOB, so a relaunch would resolve as the owner of today and replace the packet the failed run may already have written. The midday window is three passes rather than one because one could not decide anything: job_log_stale_after_s = 2200 leaves a midday that hung at 12:00 still reading as warm at 12:25, and only by 12:55 is the log cold enough to tell a hung job from a working one.

05  Morning flow

The morning relay

In plain words

The morning is a relay race, and the baton is a file. One stage writes a file and finishes. The next stage picks that file up. No stage knows or cares what happened before it beyond what is written down.

The diagram below shows the handoffs. The top row is the programs, the row beneath it is the file each one leaves behind. The last arrow on the right is the only one that crosses days: at night the system comes back to the database rows the 08:45 stage created, hours after the email has already been sent and read.

Each stage writes one artifact and the next stage reads it. Nothing talks to anything else at runtime. If a stage dies, its file is simply absent and the next stage records the absence in the packet's gaps_to_fill list rather than raising.

06  Opened up

Inside the scan

In plain words

At 08:45 everything the system knows arrives in one place and every decision gets made. This is the only part that decides anything at all. Nothing before it judges, and nothing after it changes a judgement.

It works through eight steps in a fixed order, and the order is the point. It establishes where each price came from before it calculates anything from it, and calculates before it scores, so a number that could not be worked out honestly stays blank instead of quietly becoming a zero that looks like a real measurement.

Between step seven and step eight sits a check that can kill the whole morning. It asks whether the prices really are from today. If any of them are not, the system writes down exactly which rows failed, sends nothing, and stops. There is deliberately no middle option: a stale price is not weak evidence to be hedged around, it is a wrong number wearing the costume of a right one.

The scan does more work than anything else in the system and it is the only place where a decision is made. Eight ordered steps, no model involved in any of them, and between the seventh step and the eighth an assertion that can end the run outright. After every price and prior close is attached and before any eligibility flag or score is computed, vintage.py checks five things: that every priced candidate's price_time is dated today and falls inside the 04:00 to 09:30 premarket window, that no prior_high sits below its own prior_close (two fields read from one OHLC bar cannot do that), that the prior close is dated to the prior trading session per the exchange calendar rather than merely to some earlier date, and that every market snapshot row is either dated today or labelled prior_session_only and dated to that session, and that every notable movers row is stamped with the session its own leg declares. That last check asks a different question from the other four, because that section mixes a premarket leg with completed session legs on purpose. That last check is only worth anything because of where its answer comes from. Both universe legs carry the session date the VENDOR stamped on the rows it sent, read out of the closes sidecar, and the section refuses to publish a leg the vendor contradicts. Stamping those legs with the exchange calendar's own answer and then validating the stamp by asking the calendar again would be a check that cannot fail, which is a check that says nothing. A violation rewrites data/UNVERIFIED naming each failing row and the check that caught it, and the scan exits non-zero, so nothing is stamped, no packet is written, and the morning chain stops before the analyst call. There is deliberately no degrade path: a stale price is not thin evidence a report can hedge around, it is a wrong number wearing the costume of a right one.

Step 2 carries a fill warning, and it is a warning and never an approval. A third of what the report publishes has a headline level no market stands behind, and without the warning a reader finds that out three weeks later in a database, if at all. Alpaca refuses a running session, so the morning cannot run the real check; it computes its own band from the collector's sample instead and prints it in Skips and traps. Measured over 66 rows, thin caught 6 of the 10 the night calls untradeable and flagged 6 of the 44 that were fine, and not flagged got 4 untradeable levels past it, which is why its silence means nothing. It cannot be calibrated better, and the reason is structural: the morning centres its band on pm_high and the night on entry_ref_true, which differ by a median 1.19 percent and by up to 20.9, so on the names that matter most the two bands do not overlap. A floor sweep from 5,000 to 100,000 dollars trades one error for the other at every step. So it keeps its own vocabulary, thin and not flagged, never the night's plausible, which a reader at 08:45 would take for a verdict that cannot exist until the session is over, and it reads its band width from [Truth] rather than restating it so the two cannot drift apart.

The packet also carries the ledger's counts. The scan calls paper_ledger.py's record_so_far and carries the result into the packet. It is the first thing in the morning path that reads the record back rather than only writing to it, it costs one local table read and no vendor call, and what it produces is a report section that is not about today's names. See section 11.

07  Setups

What makes a share qualify

In plain words

Twelve names reach the report each morning. Two separate tests then ask whether each one is worth acting on, and a name can pass either, both, or neither. Passing nothing is a normal result and the name still appears, described and unrecommended.

The day setup is for something to be done in the first hour: a decent move, genuinely unusual volume behind it, and a price that has already cleared yesterday's peak. The swing setup is for something held for days: a much bigger move, a real piece of news behind it, and a price above its own long term trend.

They are deliberately kept as two separate lists of rules rather than one list with settings, because a share worth an hour and a share worth a week are different animals and the two are allowed to disagree about the same name. They frequently do, and the example below shows one.

Every threshold on this page is read from CRITERIA.md at run time and appears in no Python file. A candidate is day_eligible only when every line of the day setup passes, and swing_eligible only when every line of the swing setup does. The names of any failed conditions are recorded on the row, so a rejection can be argued with rather than guessed at.

latest premarket priceabove $3above $3Below three dollars the spread between buying and selling price is a large fraction of the move, so the level printed is not a level anyone trades at.
market valueabove $1B$800M or moreKeeps out the very small companies where a modest amount of buying moves the price a long way and the move means little.
relative volumeabove 1.5xnot testedThe day setup's most important line. The move has to be backed by at least half again the trading this share normally sees by this time. The swing setup omits it deliberately: over days, the news matters more than this morning's crowd.

Every number in the worked example below is invented. The four ticker symbols are real companies, and nothing beside them is: the prices, gaps, volumes and outcomes were made up to show the rules working, and the catalysts are named only by the class the system records rather than as events that happened. This is not a real session, not a record of one, and not a recommendation about any of these companies.

A rule that cannot be measured is not a rule that passed. If the baseline for a share is missing, or fewer than ten sessions of history exist, or the collector heard nothing, relative volume is null with a written reason rather than a zero or a guess. A null cannot satisfy "above 1.5", so the day setup fails and the report names that candidate in its disclaimer line by ticker. This is the difference between a share that was tested and rejected and one that could not be tested at all, and the report is required to keep the two apart.

08  Opened up

Inside the analyst pass

In plain words

An AI writes the readable prose in this report, and this section is about the fence built around it.

By the time the model is called, every decision has already been made and written down: which shares appear, what each number is, how confident the system is. The model is handed all of that and asked only to explain it in English. It cannot add a share, change a number, or raise a confidence rating, because those are not things it is asked for and the finished text is checked against the data afterwards. If it mentions a company that is not in the data, the whole run fails rather than shipping.

One rule is worth singling out. The sentence describing what would prove a trade wrong has to be written in words with the digits left out. The reason is blunt: nothing in this system checks a number the model writes. So on the one line where an invented figure would do real damage, the opportunity is removed rather than policed. A sentence with no digits in it cannot invent one.

If the model fails or takes too long, the system writes the report itself as plain tables and says at the top that the narrative is missing and why. An email always goes.

This is the boundary the whole design is built around. Everything to the left of it is deterministic and reproducible. Everything to the right is prose. The model never chooses a name, never sets a number, and never changes a conviction.

09  The desk

The nine screens

Nine on this machine and eight in public. Since 2026-09-11 every build writes the desk twice: site/PremarketDesk.html, which is published and carries eight, and local/PremarketDesk.html, the same desk plus Health, which never leaves the machine.

In plain words

The emailed report is a document, and on 2026-09-03 it was seventy two thousand characters of it. Nothing in it was wrong. The problem was that the decision had to be found by reading.

So there is a second surface. The desk is a single web page holding every session the project has ever recorded, nine views onto it, and no server behind it. It answers WHICH NAME by letting you look. The prose still answers WHY, kept in full one click away, because explaining why is the thing this project is actually good at.

It is one file. Every session's data is compressed and embedded inside the page itself, so it opens from a hard drive with no internet connection and behaves identically. There is no framework, no library and no build step: about 570 lines of JavaScript and 300 of CSS.

Every view is a hash route, which means every view is a link. The share you are looking at is in the address, so what one person is reading can be sent to another machine and be the same thing. Filters live in the address too, so a filtered list is also a link. Back and forward work. Both light and dark themes are designed rather than one inverted from the other.

#/session/<date>/morning
1. Morning

Answers: which of this morning's names is worth the next forty five minutes, and what would make me wrong. The screen the desk opens on. Across the top, how the wider market is behaving. Then counters saying how many names were kept, how many passed each setup, how many scored green, each naming its members. Then every candidate on one chart, filterable and clickable. Selecting one opens everything known about it: the levels, the shape of its morning's trading, the score broken into its parts, the evidence behind each, the headline and what the model says the headline is actually about, and the traps.

#/session/<date>/midday
2. Midday

Answers: did the levels the morning published survive contact with the open, and what moved that the morning never named. One row per name saying, in words rather than codes, whether the entry level was reached, opened straight past, or never came near. Beside it what the rest of the market did, and what each of the screening floors turned down, biggest mover first. Before noon it counts down to the pass instead of sitting blank, and it distinguishes a pass that has not run yet from one that failed from a page that is simply older than the machine.

#/session/<date>
3. Session

Answers: what happened on this day, whole. The only screen where the morning's estimate and the night's measurement appear together, in two columns, never merged. A premarket volume the morning estimated by scaling what it heard, and the true consolidated figure the night bought from a second data provider, are two different numbers. A screen that showed one of them and called it "the volume" would be worse than the report already is.

#/sessions
4. Sessions

Answers: what have the mornings looked like, and which one do I want. A calendar of every month on file at once. A day the desk holds a morning for is lifted and clickable and carries that morning's biggest mover as a bar on one scale across all months, so a heavy morning is visible before it is opened. Every other day is greyed, at two different weights, because a weekday the machine did not run and a date before the records begin are different facts.

#/record
5. Record

Answers: is any of this working. Five questions in order: did it run, is it trustworthy, what did it publish, what did it cost, and does the conviction score actually order the outcomes. That last one is the whole project's exam. Every count arrives with both of its denominators, rows and sessions, and any group below the stated minimum size refuses to print rather than showing a number too small to mean anything.

#/name/<ticker>
6. Name

Answers: has this share done this before. Every previous appearance of one ticker, side by side, with the levels and the shape of each morning and what followed each one. On the report this exists only as a count of repeats within five sessions. Here it opens back across everything on file.

#/session/<date>/health, local desk only
7. Health

Local only since 2026-09-11. It left the published desk on the owner's instruction, because every line of it was the machine describing itself, and stayed on local/PremarketDesk.html, the copy every build writes for this machine and never uploads.

Answers: is the machine right. A verdict line, then six or seven checks each with a state and a plain English paragraph carrying the figures. The one that earns this screen its place is the last: the names that cleared the volume floor only because an estimated scaling factor was applied, and which do not clear it on what was actually heard. That is the single place anywhere that a name is shown to be present because of an assumption rather than a measurement. The raw data is still here, folded, because the working should be checkable without being the first thing read.

#/session/<date>/report
8. The report, as written

Answers: what did the email actually say that morning. The prose exactly as it shipped. The screens replace reading with looking; they do not replace the writing, and the version that went out has to remain retrievable without qualification.

#/session/<date>/precedent
9. Similar

Answers: what happened the last time a share looked like each of these. The newest screen, and the one closest to the point of the whole project. It matches each of this morning's names against every comparable name in the record and reports what those did. What counts as "a name like this one" was written down before a single base rate existed, so the definition could not be shaped to flatter the answer.

Every screen reads what is already on disk and computes nothing at view time. Each session's data is frozen once, at the end of the morning, into a compressed payload embedded in the page. That freeze is what makes a screen reproducible: the shape of a morning's trading cannot be redrawn exactly from the collector's file afterwards, so it is drawn once and kept. No screen touches the database when it renders.

Worked example

Take the illustrative SNOW row from section 07, and follow it across the screens.

Morning shows it on the chart of all twelve, marked green, and selecting it opens the entry and stop levels, the trading shape of its morning, the score split into the gap, the volume, the catalyst and the position, the earnings headline with a sentence saying whether that headline is about the company itself or its sector, and the traps. Midday says in words whether the entry was reached and by how much it missed if not. Session puts the morning's estimated volume beside what the night measured from the other data provider, so the size of the morning's error is visible rather than corrected away. Similar says what happened the other times a name gapped like this on earnings with volume like this. Name says whether SNOW itself has been here before, and what followed. Record says whether green names as a class have been outperforming red ones at all yet, and refuses to answer if the sample is still too small.

The numbers in that walk are the invented ones from section 07. The screens are real and shipped.

10  Coverage

The blind window and the night

In plain words

The morning report is written from an incomplete picture, and this section is about being honest regarding how incomplete.

Two gaps cause it. The report is written at 08:45 but the premarket runs until 09:30, so the last forty five minutes are simply not in it. And the data provider does not publish the full, corrected record of those hours until late the same evening, so nobody, anywhere, can know the complete truth at breakfast time.

So at night the system fetches the complete record and writes the true figures in fresh columns beside the morning's estimates, never over them. It then does it a second time using a different data provider entirely, as a cross check, and labels which columns came from which. The gap between what the morning thought and what was actually true is not noise to be cleaned up. It is the measurement, and it is what paid for the change that made the system start listening at 04:00 instead of 07:20.

Two facts force the night pipeline to exist. The morning's premarket high describes a window that is still not the whole session. Until 2026-09-03 that was the large hole: the session opens at 04:00 and the collector only started listening at 07:20. The clock in CRITERIA moved a day earlier, on 2026-09-02, and [Collector] two_phase_first_session is the key that keeps the two apart, because a row from either side of it was measured against a different window. It now opens with the session, and what remains is narrower but real. The window ends at the 08:45 cutoff rather than at 09:30; a name that reaches the pool only at the 07:15 pass is subscribed at the handover, so its tape begins at 07:20 and window_open_at is what records that; and a name that never reached the pool has no tape at all. And the vendor publishes its one-minute intraday bars several hours behind live, so the full truth does not exist anywhere until the evening.

A second vendor measures the same window, and it is named rather than hidden. true_volume.py fetches the identical 04:00 to 09:30 stretch from Alpaca's full SIP tape and writes pm_volume_true, pm_rvol_true and pm_float_rotation_true beside the morning's estimates. It runs at night because it has to: the free plan serves the sip feed for a session that is OVER and refuses a running one with 403, measured in doc/ALPACA_PROBE.md. A column suffix is not a provenance. The backfill's pm_high_true comes from EODHD intraday and every column this pass writes comes from Alpaca, so truth_source carries the vendor on every row. And both sides of every ratio come from one tape: pm_rvol_true divides an Alpaca window into an Alpaca baseline over the same window on the prior sessions, because dividing an Alpaca numerator into the EODHD baseline the morning uses would repeat, one vendor down, the exact defect the pass exists to correct.

The same pass measures the reference levels, and that is what the record rests on. entry_ref and stop_ref are the collector's raw live pm_high and pm_low, which are the extremes of a socket SAMPLE. 11  Record

Four passes fill one row, and a fifth books a trade beside it

Start here: what is this whole chapter for?

Every morning this system puts a number on a stock. It says ACME scores 7.0, conviction green, and here is the price it would have to clear. Nothing about that number has been earned. The thresholds behind it were copied from a stranger and have never been tested on this data. So the obvious question, and the only one that matters in the long run, is: does that score actually separate the good mornings from the bad ones, or is it decoration?

You cannot answer that by remembering. You need, for every stock the system ever named, a written record of what it claimed, then what was really true, then what the stock actually did, all three side by side and none of them able to quietly overwrite another. That record is what this chapter builds. Five programs write it, at five different moments, and everything below is the mechanics of those five.

It is not a trading log and it is not tracking your money. No trade is ever placed. The one rule played over the record exists to turn a claim into a number you can argue with, and its profit and loss is the measuring instrument, not the goal.

In plain words

Pick one stock on one morning and follow it through the database. It is the clearest way to see the whole system at once, because five separate programs write about that one stock at five different times, and not one of them is allowed to overwrite what an earlier one wrote. Everything is added beside, never on top.

Four of the five write to the same row. At 08:45 the morning program writes down what the system CLAIMED: the price, the size of the gap, the score, how confident it was. That night two more programs go back and write down what was actually TRUE, each using a different data provider, because the morning was working from an incomplete picture and now the full day's trading record is available. Then, one day later and five days later, a fourth program returns once more to write down what the stock actually went on to DO.

The fifth writes somewhere else, and that is the point of it. It takes one trading rule, written down in advance, and works out what that rule would have done with this stock: would it have bought, at what price, and what would the result have been. That answer goes into its own separate table, one row per stock per version of the rule, so a record of what the system CLAIMED and a record of what a RULE would have done never get mixed into one number.

Until a few hundred of these rows exist, the report they feed should be read as a formatted list of names rather than as a judgement about any of them.

When the five run, and which of them writes where

Three of the five fire within seconds of each other inside the same nightly job, which is the part a list of programs hides. The order they fire in is not arbitrary: each one needs what the one before it wrote.

THE FIFTH PASS, paper_ledger.py, writes no column here at all: it books a row in a different table and it is described under the figure.

The fifth pass writes a ledger row, not a picks column. paper_ledger.py applies ONE written rule from CRITERIA [Paper] to every live pick and books the result into its own table, paper_trades, keyed on (date, ticker, rule_version). A separate table and not a set of extra columns, because a picks row is one morning's CLAIM about a stock and a ledger row is what one RULE did with that claim: a count over both at once would describe neither, and the rule can change while the claim cannot. The rule was written into CRITERIA before any of the code existed, which is the only order that makes it a rule rather than a description of what the code happened to do: trigger at the first regular-session minute whose high reaches entry_ref_true, entry at max(entry_ref_true, that minute's open), stop at stop_ref_true, a minute that both triggers and stops booked as STOPPED because one bar carries no sequence, exit at the session close if the stop never came, no target deliberately because a target is a free parameter that thirteen trades will fit any value of, and a NULL P&L rather than a zero when the trigger never fired. Any row whose fill_plausible is not plausible is skipped WITH ITS REASON WRITTEN, never dropped. So is a row whose stop sits at or above its entry, in every sizing mode rather than one: that is not a trade at a bad price, it is an arithmetic impossibility, and booking it would put a number in the ledger no market could have produced.

Every rule version is booked side by side over the same trades. [Paper]'s sizing map is the version registry, so a new version lands beside what the old one produced and never over it: a ledger that overwrote itself on every rule change could not answer whether the change helped, which is most of what a ledger is for. v2 differs from v1 in exactly one thing, the position size, from a fixed 10,000 dollar notional to a fixed dollar risk. Entry, stop, exit, tie break and skip rule are v1's byte for byte, so the two book the same trades and any difference is attributable to the sizing alone. Over the 13 trades on file at 2026-09-06: v1 total +1,062.39 with a worst single trade of -386.28 on 5,610 dollars of risk taken, v2 total +295.32 with a worst of -749.94 on 9,422. Every per-trade PERCENT return is identical under both, which is the point. Sizing cannot change what a trade did; it changes the portfolio's shape, and here it has doubled the worst single loss by putting more money behind the trades with the tightest stops. The earlier reading of this pair was the opposite and is worth keeping in view. Over the 16 trades on file at 2026-08-29, before the record was cut back to 2026-09-01, v1 totalled -3,487.81 with a worst of -1,912.54 and v2 -2,713.74 with a worst of -748.90 on slightly LESS risk, so v2 looked like the safer sizing on every measure. Two samples of thirteen and sixteen trades, no rows in common, and they disagree about which sizing is safer. That is what a sample this size does. [Paper] pre-registers the verdict at 200 trades across 60 sessions, and neither of these is it.

The morning reads the ledger back. scan.py calls the ledger's record_so_far and puts the counts in the packet; the report renders them in a section that is not about today's names. That section exists because last week's individual winners and losers predict nothing about this morning. What carries something is the SHAPE of what those trades did, so the ledger records minutes_to_trigger from the OPEN, minutes_to_peak from the ENTRY, and mfe_pct_held, which is what the POSITION was worth while it was open and is not picks.mfe_pct_true. Every count arrives over BOTH denominators, rows and sessions, and REPORT_TEMPLATE.md forbids the model turning any of them into an instruction or calling any of them a pattern, a signal, an edge or a tendency. Reading it costs the 08:45 window nothing: it is one local table read, and paper_ledger.py imports its Alpaca client inside book() rather than at module scope so importing the ledger from the morning pulls no HTTP client into that window.

12  Rules

Load-bearing rules

In plain words

Seven decisions that everything else rests on. If you read nothing else technical on this page, read these: they are the shortest honest description of how the system thinks.

Changing any one of them would not be a settings tweak, it would be a different system.

Seven decisions that shape everything above. Changing any of them changes the architecture rather than the configuration.

One vendor
EODHD is the only vendor a published number comes from

REST for quotes, history, news, and calendars. The trades websocket for live premarket. Nothing in selection, collect or morning calls a second vendor, so there is never a question of which feed a number in the report came from.

The rule is about a PUBLISHED number and not about the tree as a whole, and that distinction is the whole of it. true_volume.py and paper_ledger.py are scheduled night steps and both read Alpaca. What neither can do is reach a report. Alpaca measures the RECORD, after the fact, into columns that carry truth_source and sit beside the morning's values rather than over them. It cannot reach a report, and it could not if someone wanted it to: the free plan refuses a running session with 403, so there is no version of this that runs at 08:45.

One place
Every threshold lives in CRITERIA.md

No Python file contains a screening number. The reader raises on anything missing, so a threshold cannot quietly exist in two places and drift.

The boundary
Python decides, the model narrates

Membership, eligibility, scores, and conviction are all computed before the packet is piped to the CLI. A containment check fails the run if the report names a ticker the packet does not carry.

No guessing
Missing evidence is null with a reason

A relative volume that cannot be computed honestly is null plus a reason string, never a substituted proxy. The disclaimer line names every such candidate by ticker.

No overwriting
Corrections sit beside the original

The night writes true premarket columns next to the morning's live values and never over them. The distance between them is a measurement the project needs, not an error to clean up.

One rule
The paper rule is written in CRITERIA before it is written in code

Entry, stop, exit, size and every refusal live in CRITERIA [Paper], and paper_ledger.py applies them. ONE rule, not a family: no target, because a target is a free parameter that thirteen trades will fit any value of, and a rule with something to tune is a rule that cannot be wrong. Versions are booked side by side over the same trades, each differing from the last in exactly one thing, so a change that helps can be told from a change that happened. And the verdict is pre-registered at 200 trades across 60 sessions, in the same file, before the count gets there.

Human gate
No email until a real morning is verified

While data/UNVERIFIED exists, sending is refused regardless of configuration. Only a person who has checked a live morning's numbers against a reference deletes it.

15  Failure

Failure behaviour

ReferenceWhat happens when each part breaks, one row per part.
In plain words

Every way this system can fail was worked out in advance, and the table below is that list. The question asked of each one is the same: what goes missing, what does the next stage do about it, and does the email still go out.

Almost everything either degrades gracefully, meaning the email arrives with a section missing and a sentence saying why, or fixes itself on the next run. Exactly one failure is permanent. If the 08:45 stage dies, that morning is lost for good, because live premarket trading cannot be fetched again afterwards. That asymmetry is why the 08:45 stage swallows every missing field it meets and refuses on only one thing: evidence that the prices are not from today.

No job in the data path retries another and no job waits on one. The watchdog (src/ops/monitor_jobs.py, fired every 30 minutes for two hours from 07:25, again every 30 minutes for one hour from 12:25, and once at 22:45) is the deliberate exception: it checks Task Scheduler's record against each job's dated log and relaunches only what is idempotent, at most max_reruns_per_job_per_day = 1 rerun per job per day, so a hard failure cannot loop. The nightly is always safe to rerun, the morning chain is rerun only until rerun_chain_until = 09:30 because after the open a premarket report is history, and the collector is restarted only when no collector is alive, because two collectors folding the same tape write duplicate minutes and double the premarket volume downstream. The design question for each failure is otherwise only what is missing downstream, what the next stage does about it, and whether the email still goes.
